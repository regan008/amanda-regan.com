##### In class

- **Source and schema.** What constitutes one row of your data, and what does your source actually contain?
- **Interfaces and arguments.** Discussion of the readings and of the transparency checklist below.
- **Sketch, then prototype.** Wireframe on paper first, by hand, before touching any tool. Then use Claude to build a clickable prototype from your sketch.
- **Report out.** Show your view and state in one sentence what it argues.

##### Transparency checklist

These are design constraints, not suggestions. We will use them as the basis for critique and they are a starting point, not all encompassing.

- Every view should be linkable so that a reader can cite it.
- Show the _n_. A reader should know how many records underlie any view.
- Make missing data visible rather than silently dropping it.
- Never let the interface imply precision your sources do not have.

{{< policy title="Digital History Project and Paper (70%)" >}}
You will complete a substantial digital history project that demonstrates mastery of computational methods in historical research. The final project may take one of two primary forms.

- **Option 1. Digital History Article.** An article-length piece of writing that employs digital methods to make a historiographical interpretation. This format integrates computational analysis directly into traditional scholarly writing. Examples can be found in journals like [Current Research in Digital History](https://crdh.rrchnm.org/), the [Journal of Digital History](https://journalofdigitalhistory.org/), or the [Journal of Cultural Analytics](https://culturalanalytics.org/).
- **Option 2. Digital History Project and Website.** A digital history project and website based on original historical research with a substantial written component. This format creates a public-facing digital resource while maintaining rigorous scholarly standards. Look at examples like [America's Public Bible](https://americaspublicbible.org/), [Religious Ecologies](https://religiousecologies.org/), [Mapping Early American Elections](https://earlyamericanelections.org/), or [Mapping the Gay Guides](https://mappingthegayguides.org/).

These two options account for most of the types of projects students might undertake in this course. However, if you have an idea for something that doesn't fit these categories, talk to the instructor. These are by no means the only forms of digital scholarship.

Regardless of the format you choose, all projects include three core components.

- **Dataset.** A documented dataset built from primary source research. It must include a data biography explaining your methodology and should be hosted in its own GitHub repository, which can be private if necessary for ethical or copyright reasons. The specific structure and format will vary depending on your methodology and will be discussed in consultation with the professor.
- **Code.** Computational analysis code appropriate to your chosen method, whether that is data manipulation, text analysis, mapping, network analysis, or something else. All code should be well commented, reproducible, and hosted in a GitHub repository with transparent documentation of the methods used.
- **Written component.** A substantial written analysis of 7,500 to 10,000 words that integrates your digital findings with existing scholarship. The format will be determined in consultation with the professor based on your chosen project type.

{{< policy title="Artificial intelligence use policy" >}}
AI is changing the field of digital history rapidly. You will have access to an AI coding assistant throughout the term and you should use it to help you generate code for your project as necessary. We will talk throughout the semester about what it means to use AI in an ethical way as a part of digital history research and you'll develop your own ideas about how AI should or should not fit into your own work. However, there are some ground rules which are in keeping with the Digital History Ph.D. program's Artificial Intelligence Policy, which can be found in the graduate handbook. I take these ground rules extremely seriously and any use of AI which violates these principles will be treated as academic dishonesty and handled in accordance with the Graduate School's policies.

**Use AI freely for:**

- Writing, debugging, refactoring, and explaining code. Note that you must be able to read and explain the code if asked, so don't allow it to build without being deeply involved.
- Setting up environments and resolving installation problems.
- Understanding technical documentation.
- Transcription, extraction, or other similar tasks.

**You remain responsible for:**

- Whether the output is correct, and being able to demonstrate how you know.
- Whether the method suits your sources and your question.
- Every historical claim you make on the basis of a computational result.

If you cannot explain what a piece of your code does, and why it makes the choices it encodes, you do not yet own it and it is not ready to submit. This applies whether you wrote it, an agent wrote it, or you found it on the internet in 2010.

**Written analysis is your own.** Any written analysis (aside from technical documentation) you submit should be your own writing and your own thinking. Using AI to draft that prose is not permitted. The analysis is where you demonstrate historical judgment, and outsourcing it defeats the purpose of the assignment and of the degree.
{{< /policy >}}
